KC Transit Service Dashboard

Bus fares returned to Kansas City on June 1, 2026 after roughly six years free — so where the service actually reaches now matters more than it did when riding was free. Built from KCATA and RideKC Johnson County Transit's public GTFS feeds, pulled daily, this tracks scheduled service (neither agency publishes an open real-time or ridership API — see the footer) to ask one question: is transit investment reaching the people who need it most?

How much longer does the bus actually take?

Click two points on the map above — a start and an end — to compare estimated transit time against driving. Transit time is walk-to-stop + average wait (half the route's headway) + ride + walk-from-stop, using each route's own scheduled speed; driving comes from OSRM. This only considers a single direct route with no transfer — if nothing serves both points within a half-mile walk, it says so rather than guessing at a transfer. A real trip planner this is not; a rough sense of the gap, it is.

Design your own extension

Click the map above to sketch a hypothetical route — it snaps to real streets, then estimates length, rough cost, and population served using the same methodology as the study corridors below. Potential future extensions

Three corridors are currently under active study by KCATA and the KC Streetcar Authority. None has a chosen alignment, funding, or a confirmed mode yet — what's shown on the map (dashed violet lines) is an illustrative path through the study corridor's own described streets, built by this dashboard, not an official engineering drawing. Population figures are 2022 Census ACS estimates for the area each study covers, not a ridership forecast — nobody has published one of those yet for these corridors. Cost estimates are this illustrative length × KC's own two most recent extension costs per mile (Riverfront ≈$87M/mi, Main Street ≈$100M/mi) — a rough order-of-magnitude, not an official project estimate, which none of these studies has published either.

How would this actually get paid for?

The cost estimates above answer "how much" — this is "from where," using how the two most recent extensions were actually funded, not a funding plan for the proposed corridors (none has a confirmed funding source yet).

Federal: Section 5309 "New Starts"

The FTA's Capital Investment Grants program funded roughly half of the Main Street Extension's $351.71M cost — a $174.06M grant, about 49.5%. It's a competitive federal program, not a guarantee: a project has to be far enough along (ridership projections, an environmental review, engineering work) to even apply, all of which costs money before a grant is secured.

Local: a voter-approved Transportation Development District

The other roughly half came from the Main Street Rail TDD — a special taxing district about ⅓ mile on each side of the route, funded by a property tax surcharge plus a 1% sales tax on businesses inside it. It worked financially because that corridor runs through "the most densely developed and highly valued property in the city" (downtown, Crossroads, Plaza) — high property and sales-tax revenue per mile of track.

Why that math doesn't automatically repeat

KC's own East-West Transit Study says explicitly that a TDD along that corridor likely wouldn't raise enough revenue to match federal funding — it runs through less commercially developed areas than Main Street, so a ⅓-mile tax district there raises less money per mile. The study points to city/county/regional funding or private sources instead, none identified yet. Worth reading alongside the need-priority analysis below: East-West is also the corridor that ranks below the citywide priority rate — lower commercial density plausibly pulls both numbers down together, though that's an observation, not a claim this dashboard can prove causally. North Kansas City and 18th & Vine don't have a public funding mechanism identified yet either.

A block group is flagged priority here if it's both above the median share of car-free households and more than half a mile from the frequent (≤15 min) network — a plain, explainable rule, not a hidden weighted score. Population and vehicle-access figures are 2022 Census ACS data for Jackson & Clay counties (MO) and Wyandotte county (KS) — the same three-county footprint used for the study corridors above, not the full two-agency service area.
